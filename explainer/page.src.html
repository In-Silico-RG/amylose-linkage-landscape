1 · THE TWO ANGLES

φ and ψ: the two rotations at the oxygen that joins two glucose rings

Neighbouring glucose rings in amylose are joined through one oxygen, O4: C1′ of one ring, O4, C4 of the next. Each ring is stiff. One ring can move relative to the other only by turning about the two bonds to that oxygen. φ measures the turn about C1′–O4. ψ measures the turn about O4–C4.

Both are dihedral (torsion) angles: four atoms in a row, A–B–C–D. Look straight down the middle bond, from B to C. A is attached to the front atom and D to the back atom. The angle between them is the dihedral angle, counted positive when you go clockwise from A to D. The two round drawings are exactly this view.

φ = O5′–C1′–O4–C4. Front atom C1′ carries O5′; back atom O4 carries C4.

ψ = C1′–O4–C4–C5. Front atom O4 carries C1′; back atom C4 carries C5.

Move the φ and ψ sliders at the top: the coloured bond at the back turns, and that is the second ring turning. In a real chain both angles fluctuate. In the simulations of this work φ averages 89° (CHARMM36) or 78° (GLYCAM06) and ψ averages −144° or −147°, each with a spread of 15° to 20°.

Convention: the drawing and the sign rule follow IUPAC-IUB, Pure Appl. Chem. 1983, 55, 1269. IUPAC takes C3 as the reference atom for ψ; this work takes C5, as most simulation papers do, which shifts ψ by about 120°.

2 · REPEAT IT

The same angle at every joint makes a helix, and ψ sets how stretched it is

Repeat one joint identically along the chain and the result is always a regular helix. Which helix depends on ψ.

At ψ = −170° the helix is stretched: about 5.5 rings per turn, each ring advancing 0.42 nm. At ψ = −120° it is tightly wound: 9 rings per turn, each advancing only 0.16 nm. Twenty rings span 8 nm in one case and 3 nm in the other. Nothing else changed, only ψ, by 50°.

This is why the paper cares so much about the value of ψ: it is the length of the chain per ring.

3 · THE FLIP

A flipped joint: ψ jumps by about half a turn and the next ring turns over

The joint has a second position it can settle in. φ stays about where it was, but ψ sits near +70° instead of −150°: a jump of about 140° about the O4–C4 bond, close to half a turn.

The two round drawings show it. At the normal joint C5 lies on one side of C1′. At the flipped joint C5, C3 and H4 have all swung round to the opposite side.

The two drawings above them show what that does to the ring. Ring 2 has turned over: it now shows you its opposite face. Crystallographers who first saw this in ring-shaped amyloses called it a band flip.

In a chain (bottom drawing) one flipped joint breaks the helix and sends the rest of the chain off in another direction. The more joints are flipped, the more often the chain changes direction and the smaller the space it spans. The third slider at the top sets what share of the joints is flipped in views 4 to 6.

4 · THE ENERGY

Which angles does the joint prefer? The energy for every pair (φ, ψ)

Each square is one calculation on two joined rings (methyl α-maltoside): φ and ψ are held at the values of that square, everything else is allowed to relax, and the energy is recorded. Here the method is GFN2-xTB, with water treated as a uniform dielectric medium and no water molecules. This grid of energies is what the paper calls the map.

The dark strip at φ ≈ 100° is the valley: along it ψ can go from −170° to −110° while the energy changes by less than 1 kcal/mol, about what heat supplies at 303 K. So the joints spread along the strip, and their average ψ is decided by very small energy differences. From here on the φ and ψ sliders set the averages of the two angles, and each joint scatters around them.

The two angles do not move independently. Switch on the simulations: the cloud of visited pairs is tilted. When φ is larger, ψ is larger too, by about 0.6° per degree of φ in CHARMM36 (correlation 0.70) and about 0.5° per degree in GLYCAM06 (correlation about 0.5). Removing that correlation changes the chain size by only 4 to 6 %.

The separate dark patch near ψ = +70° is the flipped position of view 3. Its energy relative to the valley decides how many joints are flipped. The third slider sets that share.

5 · THE REAL CHAIN

With heat, every joint is a little different, and the helix becomes a coil

In solution each joint takes its own angles from the distribution of view 4. The helix survives for about one turn and then the chain wanders. What remains measurable is its overall size.

The standard measure is the characteristic ratio C∞: the mean square end-to-end distance divided by (number of joints × square of the ring-to-ring distance). A chain with no stiffness at all has C∞ = 1. The paper computes it exactly from the distribution; the number shown is that calculation, redone here.

Now move the two sliders. Shifting the average ψ by 30° changes C∞ about fourfold. Going from no flips to one joint in four roughly halves it. Those are the two numbers of the paper's title. The buttons under the sliders put them where each method puts them.

    7 · THE HYDROXYL GROUPS

    Same φ and ψ, different hydroxyl orientations, different energy

    The two rings carry seven OH groups and two CH₂OH groups. Each can point in about three directions, and they form hydrogen bonds with one another. None of this changes φ or ψ, but it changes the energy by several kcal/mol, more than the differences along the valley in view 4.

    The energy map of view 4 was computed by stepping from one square to the next, and each square kept the hydroxyl orientations of the square before it. Press Lowest energy found: that is the best of 300 orientations tried at the same φ and ψ. Watch the hydrogens turn and new contacts form.

    The lower plot is the consequence. Dashed lines: the valley with the orientations as scanned. Solid lines: with the lowest orientation at each ψ. The low end of the valley moves from ψ ≈ −150° to ψ ≈ −120°, with the fast method (GFN2-xTB) and with the more accurate one (density-functional theory, r2SCAN) alike. That is point 1 jumping to points 3 and 4 in view 6.

    In real water these internal hydrogen bonds compete with hydrogen bonds to water molecules, which a continuous medium cannot offer. That is the likely reason the simulations with water molecules stay near ψ = −145°.

    8 · RIGID RINGS VERSUS REAL RINGS

    The calculation treats rings as rigid; real rings give a little at every joint

    Views 2 to 6 build chains from one rigid copy of the ring, so that φ and ψ are the only freedom. In a simulation the rings pucker slightly and the angle at the shared oxygen opens and closes by a few degrees.

    Move through the snapshots. The two chains have exactly the same φ and ψ at every joint and start on the same ring, yet they drift apart along the chain. In a single snapshot the rigid chain can come out longer or shorter.

    On average it comes out longer. Over ten simulations of 100 ns, C∞ is 9.4 with rigid rings and 6.8 with the real ones, a factor of 0.73. A second force field (GLYCAM06) gives 30 and 21, a factor of 0.71. This is the factor 0.73 that views 5 and 6 apply before comparing with the measured size.
