1 · THE JOINT

Two rings, one shared oxygen, two angles

Neighbouring glucose rings in amylose are connected through a single oxygen: C1 of one ring, the oxygen, C4 of the next. Each ring is stiff. The only way the second ring can move relative to the first is by rotation about the two bonds to that oxygen.

φ is the rotation about the C1–O bond (dihedral O5–C1–O–C4). ψ is the rotation about the O–C4 bond (dihedral C1–O–C4–C5), the bond drawn thick. φ hardly varies in amylose, about 100°. ψ is the angle that matters here.

Move the ψ slider. The left ring stays put and the right ring swings about the thick bond. That motion is everything the rest of this page is about.

2 · REPEAT IT

The same angle at every joint makes a helix, and ψ sets how stretched it is

Repeat one joint identically along the chain and the result is always a regular helix. Which helix depends on ψ.

At ψ = −170° the helix is stretched: about 5.5 rings per turn, each ring advancing 0.42 nm. At ψ = −120° it is tightly wound: 9 rings per turn, each advancing only 0.16 nm. Twenty rings span 8 nm in one case and 3 nm in the other. Nothing else changed, only ψ, by 50°.

This is why the paper cares so much about the value of ψ: it is the length of the chain per ring.

3 · THE FLIP

One joint turned by half a turn puts a kink in the chain

The joint has a second position it can occupy, with ψ near +70°, roughly half a turn away from the usual one. The next ring is then upside down relative to its neighbour. Crystallographers who first saw it called it a band flip.

In a chain, one flipped joint breaks the helix and sends the rest of the chain off in another direction. The more joints are flipped, the more often the chain changes direction and the smaller the space it spans.

4 · THE ENERGY

Which angles does the joint prefer? The energy for every pair (φ, ψ)

Each square is one calculation on two joined rings (methyl α-maltoside): φ and ψ are held at the values of that square, everything else is allowed to relax, and the energy is recorded. Here the method is GFN2-xTB, with water treated as a uniform dielectric medium and no water molecules. This grid of energies is what the paper calls the map.

The dark strip at φ ≈ 100° is the valley: along it ψ can go from −170° to −110° while the energy changes by less than 1 kcal/mol, about what heat supplies at 303 K. So the joints spread along the strip, and their average ψ is decided by very small energy differences. The ψ slider now moves that average.

The separate dark patch near ψ = +70° is the flipped position of view 3. Its energy relative to the valley decides how many joints are flipped. The second slider sets that share.

5 · THE REAL CHAIN

With heat, every joint is a little different, and the helix becomes a coil

In solution each joint takes its own angles from the distribution of view 4. The helix survives for about one turn and then the chain wanders. What remains measurable is its overall size.

The standard measure is the characteristic ratio C∞: the mean square end-to-end distance divided by (number of joints × square of the ring-to-ring distance). A chain with no stiffness at all has C∞ = 1. The paper computes it exactly from the distribution; the number shown is that calculation, redone here.

Now move the two sliders. Shifting the average ψ by 30° changes C∞ about fourfold. Going from no flips to one joint in four roughly halves it. Those are the two numbers of the paper's title. The buttons under the sliders put them where each method puts them.
